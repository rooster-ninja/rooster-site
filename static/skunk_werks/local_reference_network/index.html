Local Reference Network

Survey your own ground. Let the data choose the shape.

A self-surveyed network of stations, located with Giga Ranger time-of-flight ranges, tied to mountain peaks by star occultations, and fitted with zenith cameras that record where “straight up” points at each site. The output is a local map of the vertical: whether plumb lines stay parallel, converge, or lean.

Purpose

Every published map rests on a reference model chosen by someone else. This project builds a small one from scratch: station positions from radio timing, landmarks from star occultations, and the direction of gravity at each station from the stars overhead. Nothing comes from GPS coordinates or a map projection.

With the network in one 3D frame, the plumb lines can be extended downward and compared. The pattern they make is the result. A smooth fit gives the local curvature, and what's left over at each station is its deflection of the vertical, the small lean caused by nearby mountains and dense rock.

Hypotheses, stated before data

H₀ · flat
κ = 0

Plumb lines stay parallel. The vertical does not turn with distance.

H₁ · convex
κ > 0

Plumb lines converge below ground. Their meeting depth is the radius R the network measures.

H₂ · concave
κ < 0

Plumb lines converge above ground. A station's zenith tilts back toward its neighbour.

Decision rule: per test, z = κ̂ / σκ. Curved if z > 3.3, concave if z < −3.3, otherwise not distinguishable from flat at this precision (report the bound).

Fusion outputs

  • Local map: station and peak XYZ in a frame defined only by the network (origin at A, “up” = A's plumb line, grid north = magnetic north at the stamped declination, with the star-based azimuth kept alongside).
  • Curvature verdict: κ̂ ± σ per direction, R̂, and z for each test separately, then combined.
  • Deflection map: ξ (north–south) and η (east–west) per station, as residuals from the best smooth fit.

Data policy: raw first

Every measurement is recorded raw, exactly as the instrument produced it, before any model or correction touches it. Corrections live in a separate, versioned processing step that can be rerun, swapped or removed without touching the original record.

  • Ranging: raw round-trip timer counts, RSSI/SNR and the weather readings at both ends. The air-index correction is applied later and stored as its own column.
  • Zenith camera: raw image frames, raw tilt-sensor counts, sensor temperature and timestamps. Plate solves and reversal averages are derived products.
  • Occultations: original video files with their native timestamps, never re-encoded.
  • Sightlines: each zenith-angle reading as taken, face left and face right, with the instrument's own clock time.
  • Magnetics: the raw three-axis field vector, not just a computed heading.

Deflections of the vertical: taken as is

The plumb line at each station is accepted as measured. Its local lean, usually attributed to gravity from nearby mountains and dense rock, is noted and recorded, not removed. No geoid or gravity model is applied to "correct" a station's vertical. In the fused map each station carries its own measured vertical, and any lean shows up as a mapped residual (ξ, η) after the smooth fit, labelled as observed rather than explained.

Why raw: a correction applied at capture time can't be audited or undone, and it quietly imports someone else's model into the data. Keeping the raw record lets anyone rerun the analysis with different assumptions, including assumptions that disagree with this one.

Azimuth reference: magnetic north

Directions to mountain peaks and to other stations are referenced to magnetic north. Magnetic north moves: the declination (the angle between magnetic and true north) drifts over years, swings by several arcminutes through each day, and jumps during geomagnetic storms. So declination is monitored live and the map grid is updated to match, with every map version stamped with the declination it used.

How declination is tracked

  • Base magnetometer at station A logs the field continuously (a variometer). Short-term changes seen there are applied to every field station's readings taken at the same moment.
  • Public observatory data (Natural Resources Canada runs geomagnetic observatories in the region, e.g. Meanook, Alberta) as a cross-check on the base station.
  • Star-based check: occultation timings give each peak's direction relative to the stars. Comparing that with the magnetic heading to the same peak measures the declination at your own station, directly.

Scale of the effect: a 0.1° azimuth error is about 96 m sideways at 55 km. Magnetic azimuth sets the orientation of the map. It does not enter the curvature result: κ depends on distances and on the angles between verticals, which don't change when the whole map is rotated.

Numerics check · same baseline, different arithmetic

Single-precision arithmetic (as on an ESP32 or a float32 LoRa frame) loses the signal when two nearly equal numbers are subtracted. See Numerical hygiene.

Model notes: stations sit on a north–south line at random heights of 1,000–1,800 m. Refraction is the same physics in both worlds (ray curvature = k / 6,371 km). Deflections are random per station and shared by both tests, because both are levelled to the same local plumb line. The analysis never sees the world setting.

Limits: what this system cannot prove about itself

Gödel's incompleteness theorems are about formal mathematical systems, not field instruments, but their lesson carries over as an engineering rule: a measuring system cannot certify its own foundations from inside. Every layer rests on assumptions (axioms) that the layer itself can't test. What the network can do is test whether its measurements are consistent with each other, and arrange the tests so that no single axiom carries the result.

Design rule: no conclusion should depend on one column. Test A (stars) and Test B (sightlines) share only the plumb-line axiom. If they agree, the star-distance axiom and the refraction axiom are each checked by the other test. If they disagree, the disagreement tells you which axiom to examine. The simulation shows this in the “Agreement” panel.

Some things stay outside the system no matter what: the clocks, the speed of light, the laws of optics. These are tested elsewhere, in laboratories and by other people, and this project inherits them. The fair claim is conditional: given these stated axioms, the network's data prefer this geometry by this many standard deviations.

Numerical hygiene: catastrophic cancellation

The signals here are small differences between large numbers. 55 km of curvature is a 0.17 m difference between an arc and a chord, a 237 m drop computed from radii of 6.37 million metres, and a 30′ excess in a sum of two angles near 90°. Subtracting nearly equal floating-point numbers throws away most of their significant digits. In single precision (float32, about 7 digits) that can erase the entire signal and make curved data look flat.

Example: computing the angle between two sites 1 km apart with float32 acos returns exactly 0: cos γ rounds to 1.0, so the arithmetic itself reports a flat Earth. The live numerics table in the simulation shows this for whatever baseline is selected.

Scenario: cancellation that invents curvature

The error can also run the other way. The radio-only test asks whether a middle station M sits above the straight line between A and B, using only the three ranged distances. The textbook route is Heron's formula for the triangle's area, then height = 2×area / AB. For three stations in a line, that triangle is a needle: s − c is the difference of two numbers near 55,000 m, and the true answer is zero.

  • Rounding or noise makes s − c slightly positive or negative. Negative gives the square root of a negative number (NaN); positive gives a real “height”.
  • Square-root amplification: the fake height is about √(AB × error). An error of 3 cm becomes a bulge of about 40 m at 55 km, which is a sagitta pointing to R ≈ 9,000 km.
  • The trap: a pipeline that quietly drops the NaN cases keeps only the positive ones, so flat data come out consistently curved. Every individual calculation is “correct”; the curvature comes from cancellation plus a filter.

Fix: never take the square root of a quantity that can legitimately be zero or negative. Keep the signed product s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c) (computed with Kahan's ordering so rounding stays small), average the signed h² across measurement sets, and report it with its uncertainty. Negative results are data, not errors. Then flat data average to zero, and only a real bulge survives.
